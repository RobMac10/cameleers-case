A goldfields track, Western Australia

The 1890s

The dust is the colour of the late sun. A string of camels comes in along the track, unhurried, each one loaded. The men walk at their shoulders the way they have walked for days.

Someone has been waiting for the water, the flour, the mail. Someone is glad to see them.

Someone else is already talking about getting them off the field.

The men who can take a load where horses fail have opened tracks through this country. The question in this file is whether the country opened to them.

The ‘Afghan’ cameleers

Were they welcomed, or pushed out?

What is agreed

From the 1860s, cameleers worked camel transport in the Australian inland. Settlers often called them “Afghans.” Many came from Afghanistan, Baluchistan, the Punjab and neighbouring regions. The label was a settler word, not a precise map of where every man was born.

In 1862 Sir Thomas Elder imported camels from India into South Australia. In a lecture written in 1879 he said he had “partly the furtherance of this object in view” — the exploration of the interior — when he imported them, and that the camels had “increased in number from 110 to 500 or 600.” Cameleers working with those animals carted materials for inland work, including the Overland Telegraph Line, built in 1870–72.

Faiz Mahomet managed Elder Smith & Co.’s camel station at Hergott Springs, later called Marree. Sources disagree on when he first arrived in Australia: about 1870, in the Australian Dictionary of Biography; 31 December 1865, in some later accounts. With his younger brother Tagh he moved to the Western Australian goldfields in 1892 and set up camel stations and stores in Coolgardie, Geraldton, Cue, Day Dawn and Mullewa.

In 1895 Faiz petitioned for the protection of his countrymen’s lives and property against threats from Europeans. In 1896 his request for naturalization was refused. On 10 January 1896 Tagh was murdered while at prayer in the Coolgardie Mosque. On 13 November 1905 Faiz laid the foundation stone of the Perth Mosque. He later returned to India, and died in 1910.

Mohamed Hasan Musakhan — also called Hassan Musa Khan — worked for the Mahomet brothers and wrote to Western Australian newspapers. On 24 April 1895 the Daily News printed his letter “The Anti-Asiatic League.” On 25 April 1895 The West Australian printed another letter from him.

Frederick Charles Burleigh Vosper, editor of the Coolgardie Miner, helped to found the Anti-Asiatic League at Coolgardie in December 1894. On 16 June 1894 the Coolgardie Miner printed an editorial headed “The beginning of trouble.” It said the goldfield wanted camels, but not “the Asiatic driver.”

The Immigration Restriction Act 1901 used a dictation test to keep many non-European people out. Cameleers already in Australia were often refused naturalization. Men who left to visit family could be refused re-entry if they failed the test or lacked the right certificate. In 1900 Faiz had permission from the Forrest government to import camels and drivers; a later government withdrew it. A 1902 select committee urged the Western Australian government to take responsibility for having granted permission and then withdrawn it.

Cameleers also worked on the Canning Stock Route in Western Australia. How that route was surveyed, and how water was found, involved Aboriginal people. That part of the story is flagged in the Teacher Note, and is not treated as settled on this page.

From the 1920s, motor trucks increasingly took the work that camel strings had done. Many cameleers left. Some stayed, married, and their families remain in Australia.

Place and time

    Mohamed Hasan Musakhan

    Clerk, writer and community spokesman · Worked for Faiz and Tagh Mahomet · Letters, 1895

    A highly educated man from Karachi, he wrote to the newspapers to defend Afghan and Indian camel men, and to protest against the Anti-Asiatic League.

    Source. Hassan Musa Khan, “The Anti-Asiatic League,” Daily News (Perth), 24 April 1895, p. 3, as quoted in later historical work; and “To the Editor,” The West Australian, 25 April 1895, p. 6, as later historians quote it. The full citation, and a note that this file has not re-transcribed every Trove line, is in the Teacher Note. The photograph is a real portrait, early twentieth century, Wikimedia Commons, public domain. No likeness has been invented.

    What they said. In the Daily News of 24 April 1895 he wrote that, from the addresses of Mr Vosper and others at the Anti-Asiatic League, “it is evident that the object of the league is to prevent the influx into W.A. of Chinese, Hindoos and Mohammedans in particular.” He went on: “It is the religious prejudice and not the colour prejudice that has prompted certain individuals to organise an association under the name of the so-called Anti-Asiatic League.” In The West Australian the next day, as later historians quote him, he pointed to the useful services of Afghan and Indian camel men on the goldfields, and said they would insist on their just demands as loyal subjects of Her Majesty.

    Could they really have seen it? He was in Western Australia, he worked in the Mahomet brothers’ business, and he could read and write in English. He could describe how the League’s speeches sounded to the men he spoke for. He was a spokesman, not every cameleer. A later portrait cannot prove what he wrote in 1895. Whether “religious prejudice” or “colour prejudice” was the League’s main drive is something students can test against Vosper’s own words.

    Why might they say it? To answer a public campaign that wanted “Asiatic” men kept out, and to claim the rights of British subjects. A letter to a newspaper is evidence of a protest. It is also one man’s wording, written to persuade.

    Sir Thomas Elder

    Pastoralist and camel importer, South Australia · Died 1897

    He brought camels into South Australia, and cameleers worked on his stations. Without those animals and those men, much inland carting would have waited for the truck.

    Source. From a manuscript lecture of 1879, as quoted by the University of Adelaide: “The exploration of the interior is a subject which has engaged my attention for a number of years, and I had partly the furtherance of this object in view when in 1862 I imported camels from India, which have now become quite acclimatized in South Australia, and have increased in number from 110 to 500 or 600.” His employment of cameleers, including Faiz Mahomet at Hergott Springs, is documented in the Australian Dictionary of Biography entry for Faiz Mahomet. This card does not invent further sentences for Elder. The photograph is by Duryea, about 1887, State Library of South Australia B 11292, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain. No likeness has been invented.

    What they said. In 1879 he wrote that he had imported camels from India in 1862, partly to help the exploration of the interior, and that the herd had grown from 110 to 500 or 600. The record also shows that cameleers, including Faiz Mahomet, worked at his firm’s camel station at Hergott Springs. This file does not put extra words in his mouth about those men.

    Could they really have seen it? He could speak about why he imported the camels, and about what they were for. He was an employer and an investor, writing years after 1862. He was not walking every camel pad, and he died in 1897, before the Immigration Restriction Act of 1901. He cannot settle, by himself, whether the men who worked the camels were welcomed.

    Why might they say it? To explain a business and exploration decision that made him famous. Praise for camels is not the same thing as a welcome for the men who drove them. Students can set his lecture beside the later refusals of naturalization.

    Frederick C. B. Vosper

    Editor, Coolgardie Miner · Founder, Anti-Asiatic League, 1894 · Died 1901

    He used a goldfields newspaper, and a new league, to argue that Asian workers should be kept off the fields.

    Source. Editorial, “The beginning of trouble,” Coolgardie Miner, 16 June 1894, as quoted in Nahid Afrose Kabir, “The Culture of Mobile Lifestyle,” Continuum 23, no. 6 (2009), 797. The foundation of the Anti-Asiatic League at Coolgardie in December 1894 is documented in biographical accounts of Vosper. The photograph was published in W. B. Kimberly, History of West Australia (1897). Wikimedia Commons, public domain. No likeness has been invented.

    What they said. In the Coolgardie Miner of 16 June 1894 he printed: “We want camels, but we do not want the Asiatic driver. There are plenty of white men able to steer the ‘desert ships’ from the outer edges of civilization to any point.” The same editorial, as later historians quote it, called for “the protection of our Anglo-Saxon race” and used the camel command “ooshtah,” which it said meant “lay down,” adding, “we have no use for you at present.” The fuller wording, which uses still harsher racial language, is in the Teacher Note. It is not printed on this card.

    Could they really have seen it? He was in Coolgardie, and he edited the paper. He could describe a campaign he was helping to lead. He could not speak for every miner, and he was not a cameleer. An editorial is not an eyewitness account of a single incident. It is a public argument.

    Why might they say it? To keep “Asiatic” workers off the goldfields, and to protect, as he saw it, white wages and a white colony. The League’s name tells you the frame. Students can ask whether “we want camels, but we do not want the Asiatic driver” is an economic argument, a racial one, or both.

    My finding

    The three witness cards are Mohamed Hasan Musakhan, Sir Thomas Elder, and Frederick Vosper. Further questions — including the Canning Stock Route, trucks, and the dictation test — are in Super Sleuths.

    There is no answer at the back. The records show that some people relied on the cameleers, and that some people organised to keep them out. They do not capture every voice that was never asked. Write only what these records can support. Do not invent what they do not say. “We can’t know” is an honest finding when the file will not go further.

    Why it still matters

    The camel strings made inland work possible before the truck. The men who walked with them were useful, and they were also targeted. The Immigration Restriction Act, and the refusals of naturalization, still show how a new nation decided who could belong. Families of cameleers still live in Australia. The question of who is welcomed, and who is useful only for a while, is not only a question about the 1890s.

    Super Sleuths

    Teachers should follow AIATSIS guidance and local community protocols wherever this file touches First Nations histories. Amber marks what is still disputed, or what later writers added.

    1. The opening scene is written for this file

    The typographic page is a constructed scene, not a diary and not a quotation. No named person is the narrator. The dust, the track, and the late sun are written to show a feeling. What can be agreed sits on the next pages, in green.

    Question. Why might a case file open with a scene, and what must you still check before you treat a sentence on that page as a fact?

    2. When did Faiz Mahomet arrive?

    The Australian Dictionary of Biography says he arrived about 1870. Some later accounts give 31 December 1865. This file records both, and does not choose. His later work on the Western Australian goldfields, from 1892, is not in doubt in the same way.

    Question. What difference, if any, does the arrival date make to the case question — and what does it tell you about using a single biography as if it were settled?

    3. “Afghan” was a settler label

    Men came from Afghanistan, Baluchistan, the Punjab and neighbouring regions. Newspapers and officials often flattened that into “Afghan,” or sometimes “Hindoo,” “Indian,” or “Turk.” Musakhan himself wrote of “Afghan and Indian camel men.”

    Question. What is lost when a file, or a newspaper, uses one word for men who had come from many places?

    4. The harsher lines in Vosper’s editorial

    The Coolgardie Miner of 16 June 1894, as quoted by Kabir (2009), also said: “We fear that a low degenerate mongrel race of human beings will follow where they lead, and for the protection of our Anglo-Saxon race we say and say emphatically ‘ooshtah’…” That sentence is kept off the witness card. Teachers may choose to unpack it here. It is racist language, printed as an editorial, and it is part of the record.

    Question. If a source is ugly, what is the difference between quoting it to understand a campaign, and repeating it as if it were ordinary talk?

    5. The Canning Stock Route

    Cameleers worked on the Canning Stock Route. The survey and well-sinking also involved Aboriginal people, including forced guiding and serious harm. This file does not pretend to tell that story on the student pages. The student pages use published, cited records. Teachers should follow AIATSIS guidance and local community protocols.

    Question. Why might a case about cameleers still have to leave part of the route story open, and who should be consulted before it is taught in full?

    6. The dictation test and the door that closed

    The Immigration Restriction Act 1901 did not always name a race in the test itself. Officials could choose the language of the test. Cameleers who had lived in Australia for years were still refused naturalization, as Faiz Mahomet was in 1896, even before the Commonwealth Act. After 1901, leaving to visit family could mean not getting back in.

    Question. Is a law that never uses the word “Afghan” still a way of pushing people out? What evidence in this file helps you answer?

    7. Trucks, and what “the period of usefulness” meant

    From the 1920s motor trucks took over much of the inland carting. Some later writers have used phrases such as “the period of usefulness has passed.” Usefulness to a colony is not the same thing as belonging in it. Some cameleers left; some stayed; some married European or Aboriginal women, and their families remain.

    Question. If a group is valued while their work is needed, and shunned when the work changes, what does that tell you about whether they were ever welcomed?

    8. Sure, maybe, or a guess?

    Sure: Elder imported camels in 1862; Faiz and Tagh were on the Western Australian goldfields from 1892; Vosper’s paper printed the 16 June 1894 editorial; the Immigration Restriction Act was passed in 1901. Maybe: the exact wording of every line of Musakhan’s 25 April letter, until a teacher checks the Trove page word for word. A guess: how any one unnamed cameleer felt on a particular night on the track. The opening scene is not evidence.

    Question. Take one sentence from the opener, one from “What is agreed,” and one from a witness card. Mark each sure, maybe, or a guess — and say why.

    Teacher notes

    What’s true about the opener

    The typographic page is a constructed scene, written for this file. It is not a quotation, and not a named person’s diary. The handover line about tracks and whether the country opened to the cameleers is this file’s framing. What’s true sits in “What is agreed,” the witness cards, and this note.

    Curriculum

    The brief asked for an accurate AC9HS5 code. The lead code is AC9HS5K03: the role of a significant individual or group, including those who migrated to Australia, in the development of events in an Australian colony. The Immigration Restriction Act and citizenship after Federation also sit under AC9HS6K02. Both codes are used. There is no Year 5–6 “AC9HS9” code; that numbering belongs to later History courses, which use AC9HH.

    Source conflicts, flagged

    Faiz Mahomet’s arrival is given as about 1870 (ADB) and as 31 December 1865 (some later accounts). Musakhan’s year of death is given as 1939 in some accounts and 1945 in others. The 25 April 1895 West Australian wording is taken from later historical quotation, not from a fresh Trove transcription by this file; it should be checked if a teacher has the page. The “Sons of God” sentence sometimes attached to Faiz after the Coolgardie food shortage of 1893 is cited to Brian Willis (1992) in later retellings. This file has not verified that sentence in a primary source, and does not use it on student pages.

    First Nations, and consultation

    Teachers should follow AIATSIS guidance and local community protocols. The Canning Stock Route, and marriages with Aboriginal women, are First Nations histories as well as cameleer histories. Teach with AIATSIS guidance. Do not treat the stock-route survey and well-sinking as a colourful extra. The student pages name the route and leave the harm in this note, on purpose.

    Religion, names, and care

    Most of the cameleers were Muslim. Teach the faith with respect. The word “Afghan” is explained as a settler label. Vosper’s editorial uses racist language; the harshest sentence is kept for this note and for Super Sleuths, so Year 5–6 teachers can decide how to handle it.
